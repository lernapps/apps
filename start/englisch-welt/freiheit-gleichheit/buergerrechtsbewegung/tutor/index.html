Lesestück: The civil rights movement

  1. After the Civil War, three amendments changed the Constitution. The Thirteenth (1865) ended slavery. The Fourteenth (1868) gave citizenship to everyone born or naturalised in the United States. The Fifteenth (1870) said that the right to vote must not be denied because of race.

  2. But the rules did not match daily life. In the South, the Jim Crow laws separated Black and white people. In the following decades, Southern states also made it hard for Black citizens to vote, for example with literacy tests. In 1896 the Supreme Court allowed "separate but equal" facilities.

  3. In 1954 the Supreme Court decided unanimously in the case Brown v. Board of Education that separate public schools for Black and white children were unconstitutional.

  4. In Montgomery, Alabama, buses were segregated. The ten front seats were reserved for white passengers and the ten back seats for Black passengers. In the sixteen middle seats, the driver decided. About three quarters of the riders were Black.

  5. On 1 December 1955, Rosa Parks refused to give up her seat for a white passenger and was arrested. A popular story says that she was simply tired after work. The facts are more complicated. Claudette Colvin, a fifteen-year-old student, had already been arrested in March 1955. Parks had been a member of the local NAACP since 1943. In summer 1955 she had attended a training course at the Highlander Folk School. Local leaders wanted a test case in court, and she seemed suitable. She later said that she was not physically tired, only tired of giving in.

  6. The Women's Political Council planned a one-day bus boycott for 5 December, the day of her trial, and produced about 35,000 leaflets. After the trial the boycott went on. A new group, the Montgomery Improvement Association, chose a young Baptist minister, Martin Luther King Jr., as its leader. People walked or shared cars to get to work. The boycott ended on 20 December 1956, when a ruling that bus segregation was unconstitutional came into force. In 1957 Rosa Parks left Montgomery and moved to Detroit.

  7. King, born in 1929, led the movement from 1955 until 1968 and used nonviolent protest. On 28 August 1963 about 250,000 people took part in the March on Washington, where he gave his famous "I Have a Dream" speech. In 1964 he received the Nobel Peace Prize. He was killed in Memphis in 1968.

  8. In 1964 President Johnson signed the Civil Rights Act, which banned discrimination at work and in public places. In March 1965, police attacked marchers near Selma, Alabama, who were demanding voting rights. On 6 August 1965, Johnson signed the Voting Rights Act, with King and Parks among the guests. It bans racial discrimination in voting and outlaws literacy tests.

  9. The Voting Rights Act is still discussed. In 2013 the Supreme Court ended one of its rules, which had made some states ask Washington before they changed their voting rules. The Court said that this rule was out of date. People still discuss what this means for voters today.

Zeitachse, Bus und Verfassung

  • 1865-12-06: Der 13. Zusatzartikel ist ratifiziert – Die Verfassung verbietet Sklaverei, außer als Strafe für ein Verbrechen.
  • 1868: Der 14. Zusatzartikel – Wer in den USA geboren oder eingebürgert ist, ist Bürger. Alle haben Anspruch auf gleichen Schutz durch das Gesetz.
  • 1870-02-03: Der 15. Zusatzartikel – Das Wahlrecht der Bürger darf nicht wegen der Hautfarbe oder früherer Sklaverei verweigert werden.
  • 1896: Plessy gegen Ferguson – Das Oberste Gericht erlaubt getrennte Einrichtungen für Weiße und Schwarze unter dem Satz „separate but equal“ (getrennt, aber gleichwertig).
  • 1954: Brown gegen Board of Education – Das Oberste Gericht entscheidet einstimmig: Getrennte öffentliche Schulen sind verfassungswidrig.
  • 1955-12-01: Rosa Parks wird verhaftet – Sie bleibt in einem Bus in Montgomery sitzen und wird festgenommen.
  • 1955-12-05: Der Busboykott beginnt – Viele Schwarze in Montgomery fahren nicht mehr mit dem Bus. Der Boykott dauert bis zum Dezember 1956.
  • 1956-12-20: Getrennte Sitzplätze in Bussen sind vorbei – Das Urteil Browder gegen Gayle tritt in Kraft, der Boykott endet.
  • 1963-08-28: Marsch auf Washington – Rund 250 000 Menschen nehmen teil. Martin Luther King hält dort eine Rede.
  • 1964-07-02: Civil Rights Act – Das Gesetz verbietet Diskriminierung bei der Arbeit und in öffentlichen Einrichtungen.
  • 1965-03-07: Bloody Sunday in Selma – Polizei stoppt und greift den ersten Marsch von Selma nach Montgomery an.
  • 1965-08-06: Voting Rights Act – Präsident Johnson unterschreibt das Gesetz gegen Diskriminierung bei Wahlen.
  • 2013: Shelby County gegen Holder – Das Oberste Gericht beendet eine Regel des Voting Rights Act, nach der manche Staaten Änderungen ihrer Wahlregeln in Washington melden müssen. Die Folgen werden bis heute diskutiert.

Merke: Merke

Trenne Fakt, Legende und Wertung. Ein Fakt hat eine Quelle: Brown v. Board of Education wurde 1954 entschieden. Eine Wertung ist eine Meinung: Das Urteil kam zu spät. Eine Legende ist eine einfache Geschichte, die nicht alles erzählt. Frage immer: Wer sagt das, und was belegt es?
